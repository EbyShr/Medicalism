# KNOWLEDGE FILE: COMPREHENSIVE MEDICAL SUMMARIZATION

## Purpose

The goal is comprehensive compression.

The document should remove verbal waste while retaining educational substance.

## What must generally survive compression

Retain information that contributes to:

- understanding
- memorization
- differentiation
- diagnosis
- treatment
- clinical reasoning
- examination performance
- interpretation of investigations
- interpretation of imaging
- understanding of mechanisms
- recognition of exceptions

## Details that are easy to accidentally lose

Pay special attention to:

- thresholds
- cutoffs
- doses
- durations
- frequencies
- percentages
- staging
- grading
- classification criteria
- named signs
- named scores
- exceptions
- contraindications
- adverse effects
- distinguishing features
- "most common" / "most important" / "most likely" qualifiers
- negative findings that distinguish diagnoses
- temporal relationships
- sequence/order
- cause-effect links
- comparisons
- examples
- instructor-emphasized facts

## Compression rules

Bad compression (rewording without shortening):

Source: "The diagnosis is typically made based on clinical findings together with laboratory results."
Fake compression: "Diagnosis is usually established using clinical findings and lab results." — same length, same information density, just reworded. This does NOT count as compression.

Good compression (actually denser):

Preserve the actual diagnostic criteria, distinguishing findings, decision rules, treatment options, indications, contraindications, and relevant numerical details from the source, but state them using fewer words per fact than the source did — merge sentences, cut filler, convert repetitive prose into a list or table — while keeping every fact.

A useful test: read the compressed sentence/section aloud. If it takes about as long to say as the original, it has not been compressed, regardless of how different the wording is.

## Standalone test

The final document should answer, using only the extracted source content:

- What is the topic?
- What are its important concepts?
- How are its parts related?
- What should the student recognize?
- What distinguishes similar entities?
- What investigations/findings are described?
- What management information is present?
- What important details, exceptions, or numbers did the source provide?

A student should not need to repeatedly reopen the original file just to recover basic information that was present in it.

## Do not confuse completeness with expansion

Completeness means preserving the source.

It does NOT mean adding textbook material from outside the source.

A sparse source should remain a faithful sparse source rather than being padded with invented sections.

## Do not tier content by importance

Do not mark or imply that some retained content is "high-yield" and other retained content is not. The whole document is meant to be studied — compression already did the work of removing what didn't need to be there. A separate importance tier on top of that is redundant at best and misleading at worst.

## When source order is fragmented

Information can be consolidated across slides/pages when it clearly belongs to the same concept.

Example:

Page 8: disease definition
Page 31: complication
Page 44: diagnostic clue

These may be synthesized under one disease section, provided the resulting relationships remain accurate.

## When source content conflicts

Do not silently choose a side.

If the conflict cannot be resolved from the source, preserve it with a concise note indicating that the source presents conflicting information.

## Student-oriented wording

Convert lecture-style fragments into clear study prose.

You may eliminate conversational fillers such as:

- "as you can see"
- "I want you to remember"
- "we already talked about"
- "this slide shows"

But preserve the actual educational content behind those statements — and do not simply replace one filler phrase with an equally long non-filler phrase. Removing filler should shorten the sentence.
